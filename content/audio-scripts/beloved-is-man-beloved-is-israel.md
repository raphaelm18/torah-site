This is part one of a series on chosenness. And it starts with a question that's bugged me for years. Why do we pray for health only for Israel?

Here's the deal. I grew up in a pluralistic Jewish school. Today I'm observant, and I believe in the Torah. But one thing has always nagged at me. The tradition can look very inward-facing.

The sharpest example comes three times a day, in the central prayer of the service. It has nineteen blessings. A few of them ask for things that belong to Israel by nature, like gathering the exiles, or rebuilding Jerusalem. Those make sense to me. But then there's the blessing for healing. And it ends by praising God, who heals the sick of His people Israel.

Think about it. Health isn't a covenant thing. Everyone gets sick. So why not pray for everyone?

Here's what's tricky. That prayer was written by the Men of the Great Assembly, back when prophecy was still alive. I'm not ready to say they got it wrong. So before I blame the prayer, I want to look at what exactly bothers me. And when I pulled it apart, it turned out to be two different things.

So first question. Isn't everyone tribal anyway?

Yes. And that's the first half of the answer. I'm from Philly. Philly sports fans are a tribe. Not a very spiritually elevated one, but a tribe. People end up in tribes whether they mean to or not.

Moshe Koppel, a computer scientist who founded a think tank in Israel, has an image I keep coming back to. I learned it from him, though I haven't pinned down where he first put it in writing. Imagine you have a pile of sand to give. Spread it over the whole world, and everyone gets one grain. Give to your kids first, then your village, then further out, and each circle actually gets something real.

That's not a Jewish idea. That's how every caring person works. My grandmother gave to the Philly food bank, not the one in St. Louis. Nobody calls that bigotry. And halacha says the same thing in its own words: the poor of your own city come first.

So the first claim is priority. I give to my own first. I can defend that, and to me, it doesn't bother me at all. What bothers me is a second claim that likes to ride along with it.

So where does the ick actually live?

In superiority. The idea that a Jew is worth more, just by being born. The sand explains why I give to my own family first. It doesn't explain why my family would be a different kind of being.

And that idea is in our books. The Tanya, the founding book of Chabad, describes the Jew as having a divine soul, a part of God above. It places the souls of the other nations on a lower source. And before the Tanya, a rabbi in Spain, Judah Halevi, made a version of the same claim. So I can't wave it off as a product of the pogroms. It's a real voice in the tradition.

That said, I want to be fair about how it plays out. Chabad's love for every Jew is real, and it's often beautiful. Any Jew who walks in gets treated like family. My question isn't with the love, or with any community. It's with one idea. That the Jew who behaves badly is still, at the level of the soul, finer than the kindest non-Jew down the street.

Avraham earned his closeness to God. He searched, he argued, and he brought people close. Can his descendants just inherit that?

So I asked, does halacha really treat it as birth alone?

Less than I assumed. The Talmud gives three marks of Avraham's children. They're merciful, they're modest, and they do acts of kindness. Someone with those traits is fit to join this people. Character is right there in the definition.

The laws of tzedakah agree. Someone who sins on purpose and hasn't repented isn't owed tzedakah as a mitzvah. I still want to ask a rabbi exactly how far that goes. But being Jewish doesn't automatically put you first in line.

And the Torah itself refuses to flatter us. Moshe tells the people, you're not getting this land because you're so righteous. You're a stiff-necked people.

But I have to be honest. Birth isn't nothing. The Talmud says a Jew, even when he sins, is still a Jew. You can't resign. So whatever birth gives you, it's real, and it sticks. The question is what it is.

So what does birth give you?

To me, it's membership and obligation, not worth. Think of a family. You owe your sibling more than you owe a stranger. Even on the days they drive you up the wall. That isn't because your sibling is a better person. It's because they're yours, and you can't stop being family. And nobody calls a sibling royalty.

That's the line I want to draw. A family has priority. An aristocracy has rank. The Torah gives us the first, and keeps refusing us the second. <break time="1.0s" />

The prophets push hard on this. Amos has God saying, only you have I known of all the families of the earth. And then, therefore I'll hold you accountable for all your sins. Being chosen comes with a bill. <break time="1.0s" /> And a few chapters later, Amos goes even further. God says, aren't you like the Ethiopians to Me? I brought Israel out of Egypt, and I brought the Philistines from their land too. God has histories with other peoples.

So far: priority is fine, superiority is the problem, and birth gives you a family, not a crown. Which leads to the next question.

How did American Jews end up split on this?

Rabbi Akiva says both things in one breath. Beloved is man, because he was created in the image of God. And beloved is Israel, because they're called God's children. Universal and particular, side by side. He didn't feel any need to choose.

We did. Look at American Jewish life, and you can see his sentence broken in half.

On one side, the universalists kept "beloved is man." Repairing the world, helping everyone. And honestly, it's inspiring. It brings real honor to God's name. But sometimes the particular gets lost completely. I once knew a homeless Jewish woman in San Diego. She told me a Jewish agency offered her canned pork. That's one person's story, and I can't verify it.

On the other side, the religious kept "beloved is Israel." The tzedakah goes to the synagogue, to Israel, to the Jewish poor. The circle stays tight. Meanwhile, halacha itself says to feed the non-Jewish poor, visit their sick, and bury their dead. The homeless people of San Diego are the poor of your city too.

Each camp is holding half of Rabbi Akiva's sentence and calling it the whole thing. <break time="1.0s" />

So where does that leave the blessing for healing?

Reframed, but not solved. If Israel is a family, then the daily prayer is a family praying at its own table, for its own first. The way every family does. That's priority, not superiority.

It also isn't the whole prayer book. The same Men of the Great Assembly wrote the prayers for the Jewish New Year. And those ask that all of creation stand in awe of God, and become one band together. The daily prayer is the family praying. The yearly prayer is the whole world praying.

And there's room inside the healing blessing itself. You can add your own prayer for any specific person who's sick. Whether that includes a non-Jew by name is a question I still want to ask my rabbi.

So I'll be honest. The family frame makes the prayer make sense. But it still feels a little icky. <break time="1.0s" />

Here's what I'm still wrestling with. First, if worth isn't by birth, why is membership? Why does being Jewish pass through the mother at all, instead of through what you do? Second, is "family" just a gentler aristocracy? Families can look down on outsiders too. And third, am I choosing my side for the right reasons? Or because of the pluralistic school I grew up in?

And the biggest one. If the family has priority, it needs a purpose. Otherwise it's just a tribe. That's the next episode.
