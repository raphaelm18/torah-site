
The Concertmaster. Part three of a series on chosenness.

I ended the last episode with an orchestra: every people its own instrument, Israel's covenant specific but not exclusive. I meant it. But rereading it, the ending felt like a shrug. The Torah doesn't call Israel one of God's children. It calls us His firstborn. So if we're in the orchestra, which seat is ours?

First, I pushed back on my own picture. Doesn't the orchestra just make us one more group? If every people has its instrument, the Amish their violas, Islam its brass, then Israel starts to sound like another club with good traditions. And that isn't what the Torah says.

Inside our own sources, the firstborn status isn't up for debate. Israel is My son, My firstborn. We're an AHM seh-goo-LAH, a treasured people, and Moses is careful about why: not because you were bigger than other nations, you were the smallest, but because God loved you and kept His promise to your fathers. At Sinai it comes with a telling clause: you will be My treasure among all the peoples, for all the earth is Mine. So the question isn't whether we're the firstborn. It's what a firstborn is.

So what does a firstborn actually do? The Torah answers this. Originally, the firstborn sons were the priests. They did the service, until the Levites took their place. The firstborn also inherits a double portion, which in any real family comes with double the responsibility. So when God calls Israel My firstborn in Egypt and then a kingdom of priests at Sinai, He's saying the same thing twice. The firstborn is the family's priest.

Put it in the orchestra and you get the concertmaster: the first violin, first chair. The concertmaster leads the tuning before the music starts, and the rest of the orchestra looks to that seat. It's a real role, and it isn't a polite fiction. But nobody thinks the concertmaster is the only instrument, or even automatically the best player in the room. The seat exists for the sake of the whole orchestra.

That gives me a ranking of role with no ranking of soul. I don't have to give up the closeness, and I don't have to make anyone else smaller. The concertmaster doesn't play louder than everyone else. The job is to get everyone in tune.

But then the next question: can't you lose the first chair? The Torah itself says yes. Esau sells his birthright for a bowl of lentil stew. Reuben loses his, and it passes to Joseph's sons. A firstborn who treats the seat as a trophy can end up watching someone else sit in it.

That's exactly the story early Christianity told about us: Israel lost the seat, and the church became the new Israel. I don't accept that, and I don't think the Torah allows it. Individuals can forfeit, but the covenant with the people is stated as unbreakable. Even in exile, God says, I will not reject them, to break My covenant with them. Jeremiah ties it to the sun and the moon: as long as they keep their order, Israel remains a nation before God.

So here's where I land, and I know it cuts both ways. The seat is real and permanent for the people. For each of us, it isn't automatic. That's the same thing I learned about earning the name Israel: you can be born into the family and still not be doing the job.

Which brings me to Max. Where does he sit? Max is not his real name, and I've changed a few details to honor his privacy. Max was born Christian, but he's a seeker: always reading, the Bible, the Buddha, whatever wisdom he can find. He spent months in the Middle East and India just to understand how other people meet God. He took his music around the country and played in hospitals for sick people, especially kids, and the teenagers who know him look up to him in a way you can't fake. And he talks to God. Not once a week, but all the time: on walks to the beach, over a meal he's excited to thank Him for, wrestling with Him when life is hard. He's close to God directly, through prayer and argument, and close to Him through people, through warmth and kindness and KHEH-sed, acts of loving-kindness, at a level I rarely see.

We were neighbors, and one day he said, teach me Shabbat. So I went over to his place. We cooked, put our phones away, and ate slowly. I made kee-DOOSH over the wine. He loved all of it, but what he kept coming back to was the blessing after the meal.

He already said a blessing before eating. Most spiritual people do. The Torah's command is the other one: veh-ah-khal-TAH veh-sah-vah-TAH oo-veh-rakh-TAH, you will eat, and be satisfied, and bless. Before a meal, gratitude is easy, because the food is right there and you're hungry. After, you're full, and that's exactly when you forget to say thank you. So the blessing before takes ten seconds, and the full blessing after a Shabbat meal takes five or six minutes. Try it at your next big meal: notice how little you feel like thanking anyone once you're full.

Max took that home with him. Converting was never on his radar, not even for a moment.

So I had to ask: is Max less than me because he isn't in the first chair? I refuse to believe that, and the tradition backs me up more than I expected. An old midrash, tah-NAH deh-VAY eh-lee-YAH-hoo, calls heaven and earth as witnesses: Jew or gentile, man or woman, servant or maid, the holy spirit rests on a person according to their deeds. By that standard, Max is holier than a lot of Jews I've met. If I had to name what he is, the word I'd reach for is tsah-DEEK, a righteous person.

Judaism already knows how to separate seat from rank. The Talmud rules that a Torah scholar of the lowest lineage takes precedence over an ignorant High Priest. The High Priest's seat is real. It just doesn't make him the better man. Max isn't in the concertmaster's chair, and he may be playing more beautifully than many of us who are.

My teacher on this, Rabbi beh-nah-mo-ZEG, faced his own Max. eh-MAY pahl-YAIR, a devout French Catholic, wrote to him wanting to convert. beh-nah-mo-ZEG told him he didn't need to. He could serve God fully as a son of Noah, and he'd be more precious to Israel that way than if he took on the whole Law. pahl-YAIR spent decades doing exactly that. The man who taught me to love the universal side of Judaism looked at someone like Max and said: stay in your chair, and play.

So what does that look like on an ordinary morning? I'll be honest that the concertmaster only gets me so far. A concertmaster plays the solos and tells the section how to bow, and that's too much. We don't conduct, and nobody needs us to reach God. What holds up is the tuning: holding the original pitch so anyone can check against it.

The Torah's own image works better, and I learned it at home. When we're trying to get out the door in the morning, my oldest is the one who gets dressed first. He doesn't boss his brothers into their clothes. He just gets ready, and it makes it easier for the whole house to move. Israel got dressed first: first to take on the covenant, first to try living a whole life around God. Done right, nobody gets lectured. The house just moves easier.

Why the firstborn at all? Look at where the Torah first gives Israel that title. In Exodus, the firstborn belong to God because He spared them in Egypt. The status comes from being rescued and claimed, not from merit. It's the same in any family: the oldest gets a different kind of closeness because he's had the most time with his parents, not because he's the better kid. Israel is the sibling with the longest history with the Father.

And the younger siblings have things to teach. In my house, the one who runs to the baby when he cries is one of his younger brothers, and he's done it since he was tiny himself. The firstborn is good at keeping the house rules. The siblings often show what warmth and initiative look like, and part of the firstborn's job is to notice that and let it move him. That's Max, playing for sick kids.

Then I pushed back on myself: doesn't that make the firstborn the goody two-shoes? In a culture that loves rebels, the one who keeps Dad's rules sounds pretty lame. But this family was founded by a rebel. The midrash has Abraham smashing his father's idols, and he left home for a land he'd never seen. The rules came after, so the rebellion could survive three thousand years instead of burning out in one lifetime. Rebels without structure usually last one generation.

And our firstborn doesn't just obey. He argues. Abraham bargains over Sodom. When Israel builds the golden calf, God tells Moses, leave Me alone, so I can destroy them. And the Talmud asks why God would need Moses to leave Him alone, unless the door was open for Moses to push back. Every parent knows that moment. You make a rule, your kid argues it well, and you're not just persuaded, you're proud of how they got there. Max argues with God too, which tells me this isn't something only the firstborn gets to do.

Where Judaism goes flat isn't the rules. It's the person who studies geh-mah-RAH because everyone does, makes meen-YAN three times a day, and prays without any kah-vah-NAH, any real intention behind it. That's a firstborn who got dressed and then fell back asleep.

That also helps me with some of the khoo-KEEM, the commandments with no obvious reason. Tefillin and Shabbat are each called an OAT, a sign of the covenant. A sign belongs to a relationship, like a wedding ring. The ring doesn't make a marriage loving, and a friend who wore yours wouldn't become closer to your wife. So the signs tune the firstborn's instrument, for the firstborn's part. Max has his own tuning, and from what I've seen, it works.

Which raised the hardest question: why is commanded supposed to be better, when Max chooses all of it freely? The Talmud says it straight out: greater is the one who is commanded and does than the one who isn't commanded and does. I've heard that line my whole life, and I don't think quoting it settles anything. You can always find a counter-quote. So let me try to work out why it's true.

The usual answer is that inspiration fades, and obligation keeps you going when it does. Fair, but that's a practical reason. From first principles, I think it comes down to humility. Doing something because you were told to means you're taking instruction. You're not a cowboy riding your own trail to God. You're a kid again, and Dad said so. And that's the right posture for a human being in front of God. Feeling like a child before Him isn't a step down. It's just accurate.

But I have to push back on myself, because the Torah does. The curses in Deuteronomy come because you did not serve the Lord your God with joy and a glad heart. Not because we didn't serve, but because we served without joy. Isaiah has God say, what do I need all your sacrifices for? Being commanded with an empty heart can be worse than serving with a full one and no command at all. I'll be honest: watching Max play for sick kids, I believe that.

The Talmud gives the empty one a way back: mee-TOKH sheh-LO leesh-MAH, BAH leesh-MAH. Doing it for the wrong reasons leads to doing it for the right ones. The person who keeps the practice without feeling it is still holding the vessel, so the heart has somewhere to come back to.

So I don't think I need to rank them. The best is clear: commanded, and alive. Next to it are two other paths with real worth. One is the firstborn who isn't there yet, but is holding the vessel and trying to get there. The other is Max's, a full heart with no obligation, and I won't call that the lesser road. Max is more alive with God than most of us who were commanded. The firstborn's job isn't to follow the rules. It's to keep them alive.

So where does that leave us? Back where Rabbi ah-kee-VAH started. Beloved is man, created in God's image. Beloved are Israel, called God's children. Both halves at once. We have a real seat, the firstborn's: get dressed first, keep the house rules, keep them alive, and argue with Dad when it matters. And the house is full of siblings, some of whom remind us what a full heart looks like.

The ick I started with hasn't fully gone away. I've stopped wanting it to. It's what it feels like to hold both halves of that sentence at the same time, and I'd rather feel it than drop one.

Here's what I'm still wrestling with. First, the rahm-BAHM's condition: he says the righteous of the nations have a share in the world to come if they keep the seven laws of Noah because God commanded them through Moses. Max doesn't fit that neatly, and I don't have a good answer yet. Then there's losing the seat: if individuals can forfeit the first chair, what does that look like in practice, and who decides? There's the word itself: is concertmaster just a nicer word for rank? I think the difference is service, but I want to keep testing it. Then, pahl-YAIR's later years: toward the end of his life he drew closer to the Catholicism he was born into. Does that say anything about beh-nah-mo-ZEG's path, or just about one man? Then: empty and commanded, or full and free? I know the best is commanded and alive. Below that, I don't know whether the Torah ranks the person going through the motions against Max, and I'm not sure it tries. And the biggest one, which needs its own post: if kashrut or shatnez really tune a person to God, why only us? And if the parts that aren't signs could tune anyone, why aren't we at least teaching them?

I want my boys to take the first chair seriously, and to notice when the person two seats over is playing more beautifully than they are.

rah-fah-EL ben av-rah-HAHM.
