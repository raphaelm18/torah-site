This is the first episode in a series on the Jewish holidays. This one is about Sukkot, and a question I keep coming back to. If everything we chase slips away like breath, what are we supposed to do with the good things while we have them?

Here's something that happens to me.

I get an amazing tuna sandwich. Or better, a jalapeño cheddar bagel with tuna from Mission Bagel. It's so good. And then I eat it at my desk, while I'm thinking about work. Or soccer. Or something trivial.

Within minutes, the bagel is gone. The flavor's already fading on my tongue. It's a memory.

And the tragedy isn't that it's gone. The bagel was always going to be gone. The tragedy is that I didn't enjoy it. <break time="1.0s" />

I could have gotten so much more out of it.

So why ask King Shlomo, of all people?

King Solomon wrote Kohelet, the book of Ecclesiastes. It's the book many communities read on Sukkot. And in it, he runs a full-out experiment on how to have a good life.

He lists everything he tried. He was the wisest man alive. He built great things. He planted vineyards, made gardens and pools. Gold, silver, servants, singers, every pleasure. In his words, whatever my eyes desired, I didn't deny them.

And his verdict on all of it comes in the very first lines of the book. Hevel havalim, hakol havel. It's usually translated, vanity of vanities, all is vanity. The more accurate translation is breath. I learned this from Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, the late Chief Rabbi of Britain. He points out that the word's primary meaning is breath. A shallow breath.

Think about it. Breath is essential. You're doing it right now. But you can't hold it. On a cold morning you can see it for a second, and then it's gone.

Everything he was uniquely able to chase, in its most elaborate form, was breath. None of it could hold him up.

So is the lesson that nothing matters?

He doesn't stop there. He names what goes wrong. And he names what works.

The failure is one he comes back to more than once. The man who piles up more and more, and never gets to enjoy it. Shlomo calls it a sickening evil. Wealth hoarded, to its owner's harm. Somewhere else he describes a man who works without end, and never stops to ask: for whom am I working so hard, and depriving myself of good?

That's someone collecting amazing sandwiches and never eating one.

So then what does work?

Here's the deal. Seven times across the book, he stops and says the same thing. Eat. Drink. Do your work. Enjoy life with the woman you love.

And almost every time, God shows up in the same sentence. The first time, he says it outright. This too I saw. It's from the hand of God. Later it's a gift of God, or the days God gave him.

He can't say eat and drink without saying from His hand, in the same breath.

So here's what I think he's trying to give over. What we control is whether we take the time to actually enjoy the simple things. Food. Drink. People. And whether we see them as coming from God.

When that bagel comes my way, the only thing I control is this. Do I take the moment to savor it, and see it as a gift? Or not?

It sounds simple. It isn't.

So why don't I do it?

Because in that moment, my mind is telling me: use these five minutes to get more done. Or: eating an amazing sandwich isn't enough. Add a soccer highlight on top.

The enemy isn't the sandwich. It's the chase for more. Even in the five minutes I have the thing. <break time="1.0s" />

And that's exactly what the sukkah does to you. It takes you out of the house. No screens. No heating. A roof you can see through. It strips you down to the simple things. So there's nothing left to do except be with the food, the people, and Him.

Now, is this just permission to kick back?

There's one verse I'd keep if I could only keep one. Go, eat your bread with joy, and drink your wine with a good heart, because God has already approved.

Already. You don't have to earn it. You don't have to justify it, or turn it into something productive first. Go eat. Go find enjoyment in this world. Stop waiting until you've hit all your goals.

But here's what's tricky. Three verses later, he says what sounds like the opposite. Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with all your strength. Because there's no action, no reckoning, no knowledge and no wisdom in the grave, where you're going.

One verse says sit down and eat. The other says get up and give it everything.

Here's where I've landed.

To me, it's a dialectic. Both are true. Be present. And Shlomo is also saying that a good life has projects we're all in on, and care about.

There's a tension in that. Our projects end with us, or they don't always live on after us. That said, a quality project maybe has some awe of Heaven in it. And that gives it lasting power.

And at the very least, it's rewarding. The same way eating and drinking are rewarding. Doing your work halfway isn't.

The bagel at the desk fails both verses at once. I'm not tasting the food, and I'm not really working either. <break time="1.0s" />

So what does doing it right actually look like?

Let's not just talk about it. Let's do it, with a date. The date is one of the seven fruits and grains the Torah praises the land of Israel for. And the lulav we shake all week on Sukkot comes from a date palm. The same tree.

If you have a date, grab one. If not, use whatever you're about to eat. And if you're driving, save this part for later.

Close your eyes if you want. Take a breath.

Feel yourself sitting there. Your feet on the ground. The air on your skin. If you're sitting in a sukkah, look up. No ceiling. Just branches on the roof, and the sky past them.

There's nowhere else you need to be right now. No next thing. <break time="1.5s" />

Now open your eyes. Pick up the date.

Just look at it. Really look, like you've never seen one before. The color. The wrinkles in the skin. Where it was attached to the tree.

Somewhere, this grew on a palm, in the sun, for months. Someone picked it. It traveled here. To you.

Feel it in your fingers. The weight of it. Sticky, soft, a little give.

Bring it up and smell it. Notice your mouth starting to water. Your body already knows what's coming.

When you're ready, say the blessing for fruit of the tree. Slowly. Mean every word. And then go straight into the bite, with no talking in between.

Take one small bite. Don't chew yet. Just let it sit.

Now chew. Slowly. Notice the sweetness, and how it changes.

Don't swallow yet. Stay with it.

And as you swallow, inside, in your own words, say to Him: This is from You. I'm enjoying it. <break time="1.5s" />

That's it. That's the whole practice. That's the bagel at the desk, done right.

Now, without moving, just notice where you are, and who's with you. Notice what that feels like.

Shlomo's refrain is never one person alone. Eat your bread. Drink your wine. See life with the woman you love. As far as I can tell, there's no one in the Hebrew Bible who is joyful by himself. Joy is always shared.

This, too, is from His hand.

Sukkot ends, and there's no sukkah anymore. There's a desk and a sandwich. So the whole thing is three seconds. Stop. Taste it. This is from You.

And the brachot, the blessings, are already exactly this. Judaism built a pause for every single thing you put in your mouth. We just stopped standing inside them.

Two questions to sit with this week. What's something you chased hard that didn't deliver what you thought it would? And where's your bagel at the desk? What's the good thing in your life right now that you're not actually tasting?

And two things I'm still wrestling with. First, I said a quality project maybe has some awe of Heaven in it that gives it lasting power. That's my read. And this book spends most of its time telling us nothing lasts. So I want to study what, if anything, it thinks outlives us.

Second, there's a real idea that we enjoy the world in order to serve God better. I don't think it's this book's idea. It turns the gift back into an assignment. This book says eat it. Full stop. But I want to explore where that line actually sits.

Go eat your bread with joy. Drink your wine with a good heart. It's already approved.
