This is part four of a series on chosenness. Last time, I ended on a question I couldn't answer, and it wouldn't leave me alone. If keeping kosher really tunes a person to God, why only us? And if it could tune anyone, why aren't we at least teaching it?

Let's start with my own objection. If these commandments tune the player, wouldn't they tune my friend Max too?

Oof, that's a hard question. My gut answer comes from a teacher of mine, a Kabbalist. He always describes the soul as a lens. When we do certain things, the lens gets a little dirtier, and the light doesn't come through as strong. So you have to polish it. That's what the commandments do. They polish the lens.

You can even guess at how. Take the law against wearing wool and linen together. Wool comes from an animal, and linen comes from a plant. Maybe the Torah doesn't want those two worlds blurred together on your body. Or meat and milk. Milk feeds a life, and meat comes from a death. These are my guesses, not reasons I received. But if the polish is real, it's hard to see why it would stop at Max.

And that leads somewhere uncomfortable. It's like being at a kid's birthday party, and some other kid is eating a ton of junk food. You think, that's not really good for you, but you're not my kid, so I'm not going to step in. Is that us? Is that how we look at Max? That can't be the answer.

So I pushed back on myself. Maybe these commandments are just extras, polish on the margins.

That was my first move. The seven laws given to Noah are the essentials, and the rest is fine-tuning. It didn't survive a minute. The Mishnah tells you to be as careful with a light commandment as with a weighty one, because you don't know the reward of either. And when the tradition does rank commandments by how serious they are, the ranking doesn't line up the way I wanted. Breaking Shabbat is near the very top. And Shabbat was given only to Israel.

So the honest version is different. These are binding on us, and optional for him. That gave me three buckets.

The first bucket is universal. The laws of Noah, kindness, prayer. Those are for everyone, and we share them openly.

The second bucket is signs. Shabbat, tefillin, and circumcision are each called a sign in the Torah. A sign is loyalty the world can see, like a wedding ring. Those belong to the household.

The third bucket is polish. Things like keeping kosher. Binding on us, and maybe good for anyone. That third bucket is where the real question lives.

So here's the deal. Does the polish work on anyone? Or only on the person who was commanded?

The tradition holds both. A medieval book that walks through every commandment keeps coming back to one idea: the heart follows the actions. And Nachmanides says the birds the Torah forbids are predators, and eating them can make a person cruel. I'd noticed the same thing. The animals that aren't kosher are usually hunters. Think about the kosher animals. They eat grass. Cows are peaceful. On that view, the act does the work, and it would work on Max too.

The other voice comes through Rashi. He quotes a teaching that you shouldn't say pork disgusts you. Say you'd like it, but your Father in heaven forbade it. On that view, the power is in the obedience.

Then I hit a wall. Any tradition with a law could say, I'm commanded too. So why is their commanded any different from my commanded? That gets us back to arguing over who has the real revelation, and nobody can check that from the outside.

So to me, I'd rather build the case on the polish. And I keep the command as what it is: our belief, as Jews, that this was given to us at Sinai. We're not here to say whose tradition is true. Nobody else has to be wrong for that to be true for us.

And here's where it comes together for Max. In part three, I argued that being commanded puts you in the posture of a kid taking instruction. Now say Max looks at a practice and says, maybe I'm not commanded, but this comes from God, and I want to do it. I choose to do it. Then he's taking that same humble posture, by choice. He gets the polish and the humility, without the liability a Jew carries for breaking it. Maimonides even rules that a non-Jew who wants to do a commandment for its reward shouldn't be stopped.

It might even answer the question I left open in part three. Maimonides says the righteous of the nations keep their laws because God commanded them. A Max who keeps them as God's word meets that.

It's a tricky one to weave. So far, though: the commandments polish us, they're binding on us, and Max can take them on by choice. Which leaves the hard one. Why aren't we teaching any of this?

I won't skip the hard sources. One rabbi in the Talmud says a non-Jew who studies Torah deserves death. Maimonides rules that non-Jews should stick to their seven laws, and shouldn't keep a full Shabbat or invent new religious practices. And there's history. For most of the last two thousand years, teaching Torah to our neighbors could get a community hurt. We were just trying to get by.

But on the same page of the Talmud, another rabbi says a non-Jew who studies Torah is like the High Priest. The Talmud squares the two by limiting both to the seven laws, so I won't pretend that settles it. When Israel entered the land, the Torah was written on stones in seventy languages, and Rashi explains it was so anyone who wanted could learn. And Maimonides himself, answering a question he was sent, allows teaching the commandments and their reasons to Christians, because they accept the text as divine. Many read the Talmud's restriction as being about deep study of the oral tradition, not the basics anyone can read in the Torah itself.

And the model is pull, not push. Isaiah says many peoples will come and say, let's go up to the mountain of God, and He'll teach us His ways. They come, and we teach. That's exactly how it went with Max. He's the one who said, teach me Shabbat.

So to me, the line isn't who you teach. It's how you teach. Picture a video on keeping kosher that treats it like an ancient diet theory, the way you'd read Socrates. That feels wrong to me. Now picture the same video explaining it as God-given wisdom, the way an observant Jew reads it, and letting the listener decide what to do with it. That feels a lot better. I'd like to think I could make that video.

Okay. But why does the tuning matter more for the firstborn?

This one took me a while, and I don't think it's about souls. It's about what lasts. If one person shines brightly, that's real. But it could die with him, or with his grandchildren. When a whole people does it, the light is bigger, and it lasts. Max's soul isn't any smaller. He's just one person.

Think of one candle lit beautifully, next to a row of candles where each one lights the next. Max is the first kind. The firstborn is the second kind, and the job is not to break the chain.

So what's Max's tune?

The Torah traces the nations back to Noah's sons, and the tradition gives each of them a role. That gets you into some dangerous waters, so I'd rather swim clear of it. Besides, the nations are all mixed at this point. Even the Mishnah says the Assyrians mixed them up. So I'll go person by person.

With Max, I'd say his chords are pretty clear. He has a real curiosity about God's wisdom, wherever it shows up. He looks for direct contact with God, on his walks to the beach. There's something about healing, through the music he brings to sick kids. And he has a kindness that faces out.

The crazy thing is, that sounds a lot like the firstborn. That's because it's the same melody. It's Abraham's. The midrash has Abraham looking at the world like a palace lit up at night, and realizing it must have an owner. It pictures his tent open on every side. What's different is the instrument. The firstborn plays the melody written down, the same prayers and the same Shabbat, so it lasts through the generations. Max plays it by ear.

So what actually changes?

Here I'm talking to my fellow religious Jews. Think about your typical Torah class. A lot of commentary on commentary. It's interesting, and it's deep, and I'm not trying to put it down. But it's written for the people already in the room. Over the centuries we built a bunker, and we had good reasons. But Isaiah calls us a light to the nations, and a bunker can't be that.

So what does it look like in practice? A few things.

First, wear the hooks. I remember when I started wearing a kippah, people used to come up to me and ask, oh, what's that? A sign is loyalty the world can see, and sometimes the world asks. Use judgment about safety, with real antisemitism out there. But those symbols are almost hooks for people to ask you questions. Don't hide them.

Second, open the table. Every now and then, invite someone from work to your Shabbat table, or to your Passover seder. That's how Max learned.

Third, teach the why, not the trivia. Nobody outside wants a detailed explanation of the knots on our fringes. But tell them what matzah means. It's bread that never puffed up, so it fights ego and connects us to humility. And here's my own read on kosher slaughter. If an animal dies in terror, that fear is in its body, and then it's in yours.

And there's one more, the one I care about most. We give at least ten percent of our money to charity. If you're someone who learns Torah all day, give a real share of your Torah, too, to people outside the room. Teach people who know less than you do. Call it a teaching tithe. That one's my own idea, not a law.

A lamp doesn't recruit anyone. It just has to be lit where people can see it.

And here's the part that surprised me. Teaching the why to outsiders makes it deeper for us. The day you explain keeping kosher to a coworker, it stops being an interesting commentary. It becomes, ah, now I understand a reason why we do this. Sharing the light polishes your own lens.

Here's what I'm still wrestling with. First, can the universal parts be taught as plain wisdom, with God left out? My lean is to say plainly that Jews receive it as God's word, and let the listener take the wisdom. Second, does the polish cover every commandment? Some, like the red heifer, the tradition itself says no one fully understood. I've given reasons for a few. I can't claim them for all. And third, if the ranking of commandments doesn't match the idea that universal means essential, what does it track? That might be part five.

I want my boys to keep the house rules, wear them where people can see, and have a good answer ready when someone asks why.
