This is part two of a series on chosenness. In part one, I pulled apart what bothered me about it. There's priority, giving to your own first, which every caring family does. And there's superiority, believing your own are worth more, which the Torah keeps refusing us. I landed on a line: we're a family, not an aristocracy. Birth gives you membership and obligation, not worth.

But that leaves the obvious question hanging. If Jews aren't better, what are we for?

Let's start with where I first ran into the other answer. The idea that we're simply different.

It was in a Tanya class. The Tanya is the founding book of Chabad. Its second chapter describes the Jew's soul as literally a part of God above. And the first chapter places the souls of the other nations on a lower source. It comes up almost right away. And the room often holds every kind of person. Religious, not religious, sometimes someone in the middle of converting. I've sat in those rooms. It lands hard.

The teachers I've learned it from are warm, and they handle it with care. But for someone with my background, the idea itself lands hard. And it's one voice in the tradition, not the only one.

So I asked myself, isn't that just what persecution does to a people?

Partly, and I don't want to skip that. Ideas grow in soil. For most of the last thousand years, Jews lived as second-class citizens, in Christian Europe and under Islam. In Europe especially, they were massacred in the Crusades, and later in the pogroms. By neighbors who called themselves religious, and acted with cruelty. Think about it. If you're the kid getting bullied on the playground every day, it's very hard to believe the bully carries the same divine spark you do.

But history doesn't explain all of it. Judah Halevi wrote the Kuzari in Spain, around the year eleven forty. He wrote it in Arabic, in one of the most learned cultures of its time. And in it, the rabbi tells the king that converts will share in Israel's good fortune, but won't be equal to born Jews. That said, Halevi wasn't writing from comfort either. The book's Arabic title calls Judaism the despised religion. So the different-soul voice is older than the pogroms. But it has always been the voice of a people under pressure. To me, the history doesn't make the idea false. It explains why it got so loud.

So which voice do I stand with? And honestly, why?

The other voice says the difference is a job, not a soul. The Rambam ends one of his books of law by saying that any person in the world, whose spirit moves them to serve God, can become holy of holies. And there's a rabbi named Eliyahu Benamozegh, a Kabbalist from Livorno, in Italy, in the eighteen hundreds. He built his life's work on that kind of idea. Israel's particularity exists to serve all of humanity.

That's my side. But I owe you my honest reasons, because they aren't only the sources. I grew up in a pluralistic Jewish school. And part of me never stopped flinching at "us and them." And this past year, someone very close to me has been frustrated with the tribalism they see. Sometimes the loudest tribal voices aren't the kindest ones. I hear that.

At the same time, I became observant. These ideas took hold of me. I'm a father trying to give four boys a sense of mission. I don't want them to overstep, walking around like royalty. And I don't want them to understep either, treating Judaism like one more club. That's the needle I'm trying to thread.

But wait. The Torah does call us God's children. Isn't that closeness real?

It is, and I won't pretend otherwise. The Torah says, you are children of the Lord your God. And Rabbi Akiva says Israel is beloved for being called God's children, and even more beloved because it was told so. There's an intimacy in the Jewish relationship with God that the job idea can't just explain away. I'll be honest, I don't fully know where I land on it.

Here's the reading I'm trying on. And it's my read, not something I received. When God first names Israel to Pharaoh, He says, Israel is My son, My firstborn. Firstborn. That word only makes sense if there are other children. Anyone with more than one kid knows the oldest gets a different kind of closeness. More responsibility, more expectations. And nobody thinks it means the younger ones are loved less.

So if the firstborn has a job, what is it?

At Sinai, God calls us a kingdom of priests and a holy nation. Most traditions produce holy people. The Torah tries for something harder, a holy people. A whole society built around God. A weekly day of rest, tzedakah written into law, and the stranger protected.

And the light spreads by being seen, not by being preached. Moshe says, keep these laws, and the nations will look at you and say, what a wise and understanding people.

My teacher Rav Guy, a Kabbalist and a good friend, taught me an idea from the Zohar. There's a difference between being Jewish and being Israel. His point was that birth makes you Jewish. But you earn the name Israel. With kindness, with the mitzvot, with a life pointed at God.

That doesn't make levels of Jews. The founder of Hasidism, and the whole movement after him, fought exactly that. They defended the simple Jew against scholars who looked down on him. A great Jew isn't the one who knows the most Talmud. It's the one with faith in God, good character, and love for the mitzvot.

Closeness to God isn't a trophy. It's a job description. <break time="1.0s" />

So far: we're not a different soul, we're a family with a job, and the job is to be a whole people that lets God's light be seen. Which brings up the obvious objection.

Wait. The Amish could say all of that too.

They could. And so could others. The Amish built a whole holy society. Muslims have a community with a law that covers all of life. Plenty of traditions believe it's their job to bring God's light to the world. And when I try to find something only Israel does, I keep coming up short.

Here's the thing. I think that search is the problem. Needing our role to be exclusive is the old hierarchy sneaking back in the side door. Every father loves his children. So my love for my boys isn't unique in the world. It's specific. These kids, this house, these obligations. Nobody calls it less real because other fathers love theirs. Israel's covenant can be specific, not exclusive. <break time="1.0s" />

Think of an orchestra. The violins don't need the cellos to be silent. The prophet Micah says it plainly. All the peoples walk, each in the name of its god, and we'll walk in the name of the Lord our God. Even the Rambam, in the uncensored versions of his code, gives Christianity and Islam a role in spreading the ideas of the Torah to far-off places.

And there's a historical fact here, not a value judgment. Ideas that started with Israel shaped Christianity, Islam, and the moral air the West breathes. Including people who don't believe in God at all. Some of the most ethical people I know are secular, and they're carrying values the Torah planted. Being the root doesn't make you better than the branches. It means you're where it started.

Fine. But does not mixing milk and meat really make us a brighter light to the nations?

Oof, that's the question I keep coming back to. A lot of our practice, especially the rabbinic layers, doesn't look like light to anyone outside. Nobody walks past a kosher kitchen and feels inspired.

The answer I've learned from Kabbalah, and I kind of buy it, is that the mitzvot work on the channel, not the light. Rav Guy calls it refining. The mitzvot polish us, so God's light shines through more brightly. The light is God's. The practice is the polish. <break time="1.0s" /> So more of Him comes through in how we act, how we speak, and how we treat people.

I can't prove that. But it rings true to me. And it means the test of the practice isn't the practice itself. It's whether the people on the other side of the window see more light.

So where does that leave me?

With a closeness I'm not willing to give up, and a role I'm not willing to inflate. Every people has its part, and the orchestra is real. <break time="1.0s" />

Here's what I'm still wrestling with. First, the firstborn reading. Is it honest, or does it just make the ick easier to swallow? Second, earning the name Israel. How do I keep that from turning into levels of Jews? And third, the holy nation. If the job is to be a model society, then how Jewish communities and the State of Israel actually behave is on the scale.

And honestly, the orchestra answer feels a little too easy. The Torah doesn't call us one of God's children. It calls us His firstborn. So if we're in the orchestra, which seat is ours? And can you lose it? That's next time.
