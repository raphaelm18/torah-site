Part 3 ended on a question I couldn't answer, and it wouldn't leave me alone. If kashrut or shatnez really tune a person to God, why only us? And if they could tune anyone, why aren't we at least teaching them?

**If the chukim tune the player, wouldn't they tune Max too?**

My gut answer comes from a teacher of mine, a Kabbalist, who taught me the idea of *zikuch*, refining. It shares a root with *zach*, clear, the word the Torah uses for the pure olive oil that lit the Menorah (Exodus 27:20). He'd describe the soul as a lens. Some acts smudge it, and the light comes through weaker. The mitzvot polish it.

You can even guess at how. Shatnez forbids wearing wool and linen together: wool comes from an animal, linen from a plant, and maybe the Torah doesn't want those two worlds blurred on your body. Meat and milk: milk feeds a life, meat comes from a death. These are my guesses, not received reasons. But if the polish is real, it's hard to see why it would stop at Max's skin.

And that leads somewhere uncomfortable. Picture a kid's birthday party. Some other kid is on his fifth cupcake. You notice, you think it isn't great for him, and you say nothing, because he isn't your kid. Is that us? "Smudge your lens all you want, Max, you're not ours"? That can't be the answer.

**Fine. So are the chukim just extras, polish on the margins?**

That was my first move: the Noahide laws are the essentials, and the chukim are fine-tuning. It didn't survive a minute. The Mishnah says to be as careful with a light mitzvah as with a weighty one, because you don't know the reward of each (Avot 2:1). And where the tradition does grade mitzvot by severity, the scale doesn't line up the way I wanted. Breaking Shabbat is among the gravest offenses in the Mishnah (Sanhedrin 7:4), and Shabbat was given only to Israel.

So the honest version is different: these are binding on us and optional for him. That gave me three buckets.

- **Universal.** The Noahide laws, kindness, prayer. They're for everyone, and we share them openly.
- **Signs.** Shabbat, tefillin, brit milah, each one called an *ot*, a sign (Exodus 31:17, Deuteronomy 6:8, Genesis 17:11). A sign is loyalty the world can see, like a wedding ring. These belong to the household.
- **Polish.** Chukim like kashrut and shatnez. Binding on us, and maybe good for anyone.

The real question lives in that third bucket.

**Does the polish work on anyone, or only on the person who was commanded?**

The tradition holds both. The Sefer HaChinuch, a medieval book that walks through every mitzvah, keeps returning to one principle: the heart is drawn after the deeds. The Ramban says the forbidden birds are predators, and eating them can make a person cruel (Ramban on Leviticus 11:13). That's the same instinct I had about kosher animals, which mostly graze rather than hunt. On that view the act does the work, and it would work on Max too.

The other voice is Rashi, quoting the Sifra: don't say pork disgusts you, say you'd like it, but your Father in heaven has forbidden it (Rashi on Leviticus 20:26). Here the power is in the obedience.

Then I hit a wall. Every tradition with a law says its law is commanded. If "commanded" is the magic ingredient, we're back to arguing over who has the real revelation, which nobody can check from the outside. So I'd rather build the case on the polish, and keep the command as what it is: our belief, as Jews, that this was given to us at Sinai. Nobody else's tradition has to be wrong for that to be true for us.

Here's where it comes together for Max. Being commanded, I argued in Part 3, puts you in the posture of a kid taking instruction. If Max takes on a practice because he sees it as God's word, and not just his own good idea, he steps into that posture by choice. He gets the polish and the humility, without the liability a Jew carries for breaking it. The Rambam rules that a non-Jew who wants to do a mitzvah for its reward isn't stopped (Laws of Kings 10:10).

It might even answer the question I left open in Part 3, the Rambam's condition that the righteous of the nations keep their laws *because* God commanded them (Laws of Kings 8:11). A Max who keeps them as God's word meets it.

**Then why aren't we teaching any of this?**

I won't skip the hard sources. In the Talmud, Rabbi Yochanan says a non-Jew who studies Torah deserves death (Sanhedrin 59a). The Rambam codifies that non-Jews should study their seven laws, that a non-Jew who keeps a full Shabbat is liable too, and that they shouldn't invent new religious practices of their own (Laws of Kings 10:9). And there's history. For most of the last two thousand years, teaching Torah to our neighbors could get a community hurt. The bunker had reasons.

But the same page of Talmud has Rabbi Meir saying a non-Jew who studies Torah is like the High Priest. The Gemara squares the two by limiting both to the seven Noahide laws, so I won't pretend Rabbi Meir settles it. When Israel entered the land, the Torah was written on stones in seventy languages (Sotah 35b), and Rashi explains that it was so anyone who wanted could learn. The Rambam himself, in a responsum, permits teaching the commandments and their explanations to Christians, because they accept the text as divine (Responsa, Blau edition 149). One common reading of the Talmud's restriction is that it's about deep study of the Oral Torah, not the basics anyone can read in the written one.

And the model is pull, not push. "Many peoples shall go and say, come, let us go up to the mountain of the Lord, and He will teach us His ways" (Isaiah 2:3). They come, and we teach. That's how it went with Max. He's the one who said, "Teach me Shabbat."

So I've come to think the line isn't *who* you teach. It's *how*. Picture a video on kashrut that treats it as a quaint ancient diet theory, the way you'd read Socrates. That feels wrong to me. Now picture the same video explaining it as God-given wisdom, the way an observant Jew reads it, and letting the listener decide what to do with it. That feels right.

**Okay, but why does the tuning matter more for the firstborn?**

This one took me a while, and I don't think it's about souls. It's about what lasts. When one person shines, the light is real, and it can go out with him or his grandchildren. When a whole people does it, the light gets carried forward.

Think of one candle lit beautifully, next to a row of candles where each one lights the next. Max is the first kind. The firstborn is the second, and the job is not to break the chain. It's what I said in Part 3 about rebels without structure lasting one generation. The same is true of light.

**So what's Max's tune?**

The Torah traces the nations back to Noah's sons, and the tradition counts seventy of them, each with its own role. I'd rather stay out of those waters. The Mishnah itself notes that Sennacherib, the Assyrian conqueror, mixed up the nations so that the old lines can't be traced (Yadayim 4:4). So I'll go person by person.

Max's chords, as best I can name them: curiosity about God's wisdom wherever it shows up. Direct contact with God, on his walks to the beach. Healing, through the music he brings to sick kids. And an open-handed kindness that faces out.

The crazy thing is how much that sounds like the firstborn. That's because it's the same melody. It's Abraham's. The midrash has him looking at the world like a palace lit up at night and realizing it must have an owner (Bereishit Rabbah 39:1), and it pictures his tent open on every side. What differs is the instrument. The firstborn plays the melody written down, the same prayers and the same Shabbat, so it survives the generations. Max plays it by ear.

**So what actually changes?**

Here I'm talking to my fellow religious Jews. Think of a typical Torah class: a commentary on a commentary, deep and lovely, and written entirely for the people already in the room. Over centuries we built a bunker, for good reasons. The prophet's phrase for the job is *or lagoyim*, a light to the nations (Isaiah 42:6). A bunker can't be that.

So here's what I think it looks like in practice.

- **Wear the hooks.** When I started wearing a kippah, people used to stop me: "What's that?" A sign is loyalty the world can see, and sometimes the world asks. Use judgment about safety, in a time of real antisemitism, but don't hide the hooks.
- **Open the table.** Invite a colleague to a Shabbat meal or a seder. That's how Max learned.
- **Teach the why, not the trivia.** Nobody outside the room needs the count of knots on tzitzit. But matzah is bread that never puffed up, a lesson in humility you can taste. And here's my own read on kosher slaughter: if an animal dies in terror, you take that terror into your body.
- **Give a teaching tithe.** We give at least ten percent of our money to tzedakah. If you spend your days learning, give a real share of your Torah to people outside the room. That one's my own idea, not a halacha.

<PullQuote>A lamp doesn't recruit anyone. It just has to be lit where people can see it.</PullQuote>

And here's the part that surprised me. Teaching the why to outsiders deepens it for insiders. The day you have to explain kashrut to a coworker, it stops being an interesting commentary and becomes a reason you can feel. Sharing the light polishes your own lens.

<Wrestling>

- **Teaching it as plain wisdom.** Can the universal parts, like the Talmud's laws of lost property, be taught as "what ancient Jews thought," with God left out? A midrash says to believe there's wisdom among the nations, but not Torah (Eicha Rabbah 2:13). My lean is to say plainly that Jews receive it as God's word, and let the listener take the wisdom.
- **Does the polish cover every chok?** Some, like the red heifer, the tradition itself says no one fully understood. I've given reasons for a few. I can't claim them for all.
- **Where exactly the line falls today.** Sharing a practice and its meaning feels clearly allowed. Teaching deep Talmud to anyone who asks is less clear to me.
- **Big and small mitzvot.** If the scale of severity doesn't match "universal means essential," what does it track? That might be Part 5.

</Wrestling>

I want my boys to keep the house rules, wear them where people can see, and have a good answer ready when someone asks why.
