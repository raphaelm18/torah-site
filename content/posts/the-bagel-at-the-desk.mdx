Here's something that happens to me.

I get an amazing tuna sandwich. Or better, a jalapeño cheddar bagel with tuna from Mission Bagel. It's so good. And then I eat it at my desk while thinking about work, or soccer, or something trivial.

Within minutes the bagel is gone. The flavor's already fading on my tongue. It's a memory.

<PullQuote>The bagel was always going to be gone. The tragedy is that I didn't enjoy it.</PullQuote>

I could have gotten so much more out of it.

**If everything we chase slips away like breath, what are we supposed to do with the good things while we have them?**

**Why ask King Shlomo, of all people?**

King Shlomo, Solomon, wrote Kohelet, the book of Ecclesiastes, which many communities read on Sukkot. In it he runs a full-out experiment on how to have a good life.

He lists everything he tried. He was the wisest man alive. He built great things, planted vineyards, made gardens and pools. Gold, silver, servants, singers, every pleasure. In his words, whatever my eyes desired, I didn't deny them.

And his verdict on all of it comes in the book's opening line: *hevel havalim, hakol havel*. Usually that's translated "vanity of vanities, all is vanity." The more accurate translation is breath.

I learned this from Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, the late Chief Rabbi of Britain. He points out that the primary meaning of hevel is breath, a shallow breath.

Breath is essential. You're doing it right now. But you can't hold it. On a cold morning you can see it for a second, and then it's gone. Everything he was uniquely able to chase, in its most elaborate form, was breath. None of it could hold him up.

**So the lesson is that nothing matters?**

He doesn't stop there. He names what goes wrong, and he names what works.

The failure is one he comes back to more than once: the man who amasses and amasses and never gets to enjoy it. Shlomo calls it *ra'ah cholah*, a sickening evil: wealth hoarded, to its owner's harm. Elsewhere he describes a man who works without end and never stops to ask, for whom am I toiling, and depriving myself of good?

That's someone collecting amazing sandwiches and never eating one.

**Then what does work?**

Seven times across the book, he stops and says the same thing. Eat. Drink. Do your work. Enjoy life with the woman you love. And almost every time, God shows up in the same sentence. The first time, he says it outright: *gam zo ra'iti ani, ki mi-yad ha-Elohim hi*, this too I saw, it's from the hand of God. Later it's "a gift of God," or "the days God gave him."

He can't say "eat and drink" without saying "from His hand" in the same breath.

So here's what I think he's trying to give over. What we control is whether we take the time to actually enjoy the simple things, food, drink, people, and see them as coming from God. When that bagel comes my way, the only thing I control is this: do I take the moment to savor it and see it as a gift, or not?

It sounds simple. It isn't.

**If it's that simple, why don't I do it?**

Because in that moment my mind is telling me: use these five minutes to get more done. Or: eating an amazing sandwich isn't enough, add a soccer highlight on top.

The enemy isn't the sandwich. It's the chase for more, even in the five minutes I have the thing.

And that's exactly what the sukkah does to you. It takes you out of the house. No screens, no heating, a roof you can see through. It strips you down to the simple things, so there's nothing left to do except be with the food, the people, and Him.

**Isn't this just permission to kick back?**

Here's the verse I'd keep if I could only keep one. *Lech echol b'simcha lachmecha, ushte v'lev tov yeinecha, ki chvar ratza ha-Elohim et ma'asecha*. Go, eat your bread with joy, drink your wine with a good heart, because God has already approved.

Already. You don't have to earn it. You don't have to justify it or turn it into something productive first. Go eat. Go find enjoyment in this world. Stop waiting until you've hit all your goals.

**But three verses later he says the opposite.**

In fairness, he does. *Kol asher timtza yadcha la'asot, b'chochacha aseh*: whatever your hand finds to do, do it with all your strength, because there's no action, no reckoning, no knowledge and no wisdom in the grave where you're going. One verse says sit down and eat. The other says get up and give it everything.

Here's where I've landed. I think it's a dialectic. Both are true. Be present. And Shlomo is also saying that a good life has projects we're all in on and care about.

There's a tension in that, because our projects end with us, or they don't always live on after us. But a quality project maybe has some *yirat shamayim* in it, some awe of Heaven, and that gives it lasting power. At the very least it's rewarding, the same way eating and drinking are. Doing your work halfway isn't rewarding.

The bagel at the desk fails both verses at once. I'm not tasting the food, and I'm not really working either.

**So what does doing it right actually look like?**

Let's not just talk about it. Let's do it, with a date. The date is one of the *shivat haminim*, the seven species the Torah praises the Land of Israel for. And the lulav we shake all week is a date palm frond, from the same tree.

Here's the practice. If you have a date, grab one. If not, use whatever you're about to eat. Go slower between each step than feels comfortable.

1. **Arrive.** Close your eyes if you want. Take a breath. Feel yourself sitting there, your feet on the ground, the air a little cool. If you're sitting in a sukkah, look up: no ceiling, just the *schach*, the branches that make its roof, and the sky past it. There's nowhere else you need to be right now. No next thing.
2. **Look.** Open your eyes. Pick up the date. Just look at it, really look, like you've never seen one before. The color. The wrinkles in the skin. Where it was attached to the tree. Somewhere, this grew on a palm, in the sun, for months. Someone picked it. It traveled here, to you.
3. **Feel.** Feel it in your fingers. The weight of it. Sticky, soft, a little give.
4. **Smell.** Bring it up and smell it. Notice your mouth starting to water. Your body already knows what's coming.
5. **Bracha.** Say the blessing slowly, and mean every word: *Baruch atah Hashem, Elokeinu melech ha'olam, borei pri ha'etz*. Then go straight into the bite, no talking in between.
6. **Taste.** Take one small bite. Don't chew yet. Just let it sit. Now chew. Slowly. Notice the sweetness, and how it changes. Don't swallow yet. Stay with it.
7. **From His hand.** As you swallow, inside, in your own words, say to Him: This is from You. I'm enjoying it.

That's it. That's the whole practice. That's the bagel at the desk, done right.

Then one more thing, without moving: notice where you are, who's with you, and what that feels like.

Shlomo's refrain is never one person alone. Eat your bread, drink your wine, see life with the woman you love. As far as I can tell, there's no one in Tanakh who is *sameach*, joyful, by himself. Joy is always shared. This, too, is from His hand.

**And when I'm back at the desk?**

Sukkot ends, and there's no sukkah anymore. There's a desk and a sandwich. So the whole thing is three seconds: Stop. Taste it. This is from You.

And the brachot are already exactly this. Judaism built a pause for every single thing you put in your mouth. We just stopped standing inside them.

<Wrestling>

- **The harmony is loose.** I said a quality project *maybe* has some awe of Heaven in it that gives it lasting power. That's my read, and Kohelet spends most of the book telling us nothing lasts. I want to study what, if anything, he thinks outlives us.
- **Enjoying it with Him, or for Him.** There's a real idea that we enjoy the world in order to serve God better. I don't think it's this book's idea, and it turns the gift back into an assignment. Kohelet says eat it. Full stop. But I want to explore where that line actually sits.
