In [Part 1](/writing/beloved-is-man-beloved-is-israel) I pulled apart what bothered me about chosenness: giving to your own first, which every caring family does, and believing your own are worth more, which the Torah keeps refusing us. I landed on a line: we're a family, not an aristocracy. Birth gives you membership and obligation, not worth. But that leaves the obvious next question hanging.

**If Jews aren't better, what are we for?**

**Where did I first run into the other answer, that we're simply different?**

In a Tanya class. The Tanya is the founding work of Chabad, and its second chapter describes the Jew's divine soul as *chelek Eloka mima'al mamash*, literally a part of God above. The first chapter places the souls of the other nations on a lower source. It comes up almost right away, in a room that often holds every kind of person: religious, not religious, sometimes someone in the middle of converting. I've sat in those rooms. It lands hard.

The teachers are usually warm and socially sharp, and they soften it well. But it's a heavy claim to drop on page two, and the room rarely hears that it's one voice in the tradition, not the only one. Imagine your first day at a new gym, and the trainer mentions that some people's muscles are made of different stuff. Maybe there's an argument for it. It just shouldn't come before the warm-up.

**Isn't that just what persecution does to a people?**

Partly, and I don't want to skip that. Ideas grow in soil. For most of the last thousand years, Jews lived as second-class citizens, in Christian Europe and under Islam. In Europe especially, they were massacred in the Crusades and later in the pogroms, by neighbors who called themselves religious and acted with cruelty. If you're the kid getting beaten up on the playground every day, it's very hard to believe the bully carries the same divine spark you do.

But context doesn't explain all of it. Rabbi Yehuda HaLevi wrote the Kuzari in Spain around 1140, in Arabic, in one of the most learned cultures of its time. In it, the rabbi tells the king that converts will share in Israel's good fortune but won't be equal to born Jews (Kuzari 1:25–27). Still, HaLevi wasn't writing from comfort either: the book's Arabic title calls Judaism "the despised religion." So the essence voice is older than the pogroms, but it has always been the voice of a people under pressure. That's my read: the history doesn't make the idea false, but it explains why it got so loud.

**So which voice do I stand with, and why, honestly?**

The other voice says the difference is a job, not a soul. The Rambam, the great medieval codifier, ends his laws of the Sabbatical year by saying that any person from all the world's inhabitants whose spirit moves them to serve God can become holy of holies (Laws of Shemita and Yovel 13:13). Rabbi Eliyahu Benamozegh, the nineteenth-century Kabbalist from Livorno, built his life's work, published after his death as *Israel and Humanity*, on that kind of idea: Israel's particularity exists to serve all of humanity.

That's my side. But I owe you my honest reasons, because they aren't only the sources. I grew up in a pluralistic Jewish school, and part of me never stopped flinching at "us and them." This past year someone very close to me has been frustrated with the tribalism they see, and sometimes the loudest tribal voices aren't the kindest ones. I hear it.

And at the same time, I became observant. These ideas took hold of me. I'm a father trying to give four boys a sense of mission. I don't want them to overstep, walking around like royalty. I don't want them to understep either, treating Judaism as one more club. That's the needle I'm trying to thread.

**But wait. The Torah does call us God's children. Isn't that closeness real?**

It is, and I won't pretend otherwise. *Banim atem laShem Elokeichem*, "you are children of the Lord your God" (Deuteronomy 14:1). Rabbi Akiva says Israel is beloved for being called God's children, and even more beloved because it was told so (Avot 3:14). There's an intimacy in the Jewish relationship with God that the vocation voice can't just explain away. To be honest, I don't fully know where I land on it.

Here's the reading I'm trying on, and it's my read, not a tradition I received. When God first names Israel to Pharaoh, He says *beni bechori Yisrael*, "Israel is My son, My firstborn" (Exodus 4:22). Firstborn. That word only makes sense if there are other children. Anyone with more than one kid knows the oldest gets a different kind of closeness: more responsibility, more expectations, sometimes a key to the house. Nobody thinks it means the younger ones are loved less.

**Okay, so the firstborn has a job. What is it?**

At Sinai, God calls us *mamlechet kohanim v'goy kadosh*, "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:6). Most traditions produce holy people. The Torah tries for something harder: a holy *people*. A whole society built around God, with Shabbat as a weekly pause, tzedakah written into law, and the stranger protected.

And the light spreads by being seen, not preached. Keep these laws, Moses says, and the nations will look at you and say "what a wise and understanding people" (Deuteronomy 4:6–8). Think of a house with its lights on at night. Nobody has to knock on doors. People walk by and see what's inside.

A teacher of mine, a Kabbalist, once told me that the Zohar distinguishes between being Jewish and being *Israel*. I haven't found the passage, so take this as something I learned, not a citation. His point was that birth makes you Jewish, but you earn the name Israel: kindness, mitzvot, a life pointed at God.

That doesn't make levels of Jews. The Baal Shem Tov and the Chassidic world after him fought exactly that, defending the simple Jew against scholars who looked down on him. A great Jew isn't the one who knows the most Gemara. It's the one with faith in God, good character, and love for the mitzvot.

<PullQuote>Closeness to God isn't a trophy. It's a job description.</PullQuote>

**Wait, but the Amish could say all of that too.**

They could, and so could others. The Amish built a whole holy society. Muslims have an *ummah* with a law covering all of life. Plenty of traditions believe it's their job to bring God's light to the world. If I try to find something only Israel does, I keep coming up short.

Here's the thing: I think that search is the problem. Needing our role to be exclusive is the old hierarchy sneaking back in the side door. Every father loves his children, so my love for my boys isn't unique in the world. It's specific: these kids, this house, these obligations. Nobody calls it less real because other fathers love theirs. Israel's covenant can be **specific, not exclusive**.

Think of an orchestra. The violins don't need the cellos to be silent. The prophet Micah says it plainly: "all the peoples walk, each in the name of its god, and we will walk in the name of the Lord our God" (Micah 4:5). Even the Rambam, in the uncensored versions of his code, gives Christianity and Islam a role in spreading the ideas of Torah to far-off places (Laws of Kings 11:4).

There's also a historical fact here, not a value judgment. Ideas that started with Israel shaped Christianity, Islam, and the moral air the West breathes, including people who don't believe in God at all. Some of the most ethical people I know are secular, and they're carrying values the Torah planted. Being the root doesn't make you better than the branches. It means you're where it started.

**Fine. But does not mixing milk and meat really make us a brighter light to the nations?**

That's the question I keep coming back to. A lot of our practice, especially the rabbinic layers, doesn't look like light to anyone outside. Nobody walks past a kosher kitchen and feels inspired.

The answer I've learned from Kabbalah, and I kind of buy it, is that mitzvot work on the channel, not the light. Picture a window. The glass doesn't make the sunlight. But clean glass lets more of it through, and a smudged window dims a sunny day. The light is God's. The practice is how we keep the glass clean, so more of Him comes through in how we act, speak, and treat people.

I can't prove that. But it rings true to me. And it means the test of the practice isn't the practice itself. It's whether people on the other side of the window see more light.

**So where does that leave me?**

With a closeness I'm not willing to give up, and a role I'm not willing to inflate. Every people has its part, and the orchestra is real. But I'll be honest: that answer feels a little too easy. The Torah doesn't call us one of God's children. It calls us His firstborn. So if we're in the orchestra, which seat is ours? And can you lose it? That's [next time](/writing/the-concertmaster).

<Wrestling>

- **The firstborn reading.** Is it honest, or does it just make the ick easier to swallow?
- **Earning "Israel."** If the name has to be earned, how do I keep that from turning into levels of Jews, against everything the Baal Shem Tov stood for?
- **The rabbinic layers.** If a practice doesn't visibly add light, is the window idea enough to justify it?
- **The holy nation.** If the job is to be a model society, does that put how Jewish communities and the State of Israel actually behave on the scale?
- **My own reasons.** How much of where I land comes from the sources, and how much from my school and the frustrations of people close to me?

</Wrestling>

I want my boys to grow up knowing they're firstborns with a job, and that their younger siblings are everywhere.
