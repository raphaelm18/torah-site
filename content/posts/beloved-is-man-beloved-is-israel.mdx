**Why do we pray for health only for Israel?**

I grew up in a pluralistic Jewish school. Today I'm observant, and I believe in the Torah. And for years one thing has nagged at me: how inward-facing the tradition can look.

The sharpest example is three times a day, in the Amidah. A few of its nineteen blessings ask for things that belong to Israel by nature: gathering the exiles, rebuilding Jerusalem. Those make sense. But the eighth blessing, *Refa'enu*, ends by praising God who heals "the sick of His people Israel."

Health isn't covenantal. Everyone gets sick. Why not pray for everyone?

I'm not ready to say the Men of the Great Assembly got it wrong. They wrote this prayer while prophecy was still alive. So before blaming the prayer, I want to look at what exactly bothers me. Pulled apart, it turns out to be two different things.

**Isn't everyone tribal anyway?**

Yes, and that's the first half of the answer. Moshe Koppel, the computer scientist who founded the Kohelet Forum, offers an image I keep coming back to. I learned it from him, though I haven't pinned down where he first put it in writing. You have a pile of sand to give. Spread it over the whole world and everyone gets one grain. Give to your kids first, then your village, then further out, and each circle gets something real.

That isn't a Jewish idea. It's how every caring person works. My grandmother in Philly gave to the Philly food bank, not the one in St. Louis. Nobody calls that bigotry. The halakha says the same thing in its own language: "the poor of your city come first" (Bava Metzia 71a).

So the first claim is **priority**: I give to my own first. I can defend that, and honestly, it doesn't bother me. What bothers me is a second claim that likes to ride along with it.

**So where does the ick actually live?**

In **superiority**: the idea that a Jew is worth more, just by birth. Sand explains why I give to my own family first. It doesn't explain why my family would be a different kind of being.

And that idea is in our books. The opening chapters of the Tanya describe the Jew as having a divine soul, a "part of God above," and place the souls of the other nations on a lower spiritual source. Before the Tanya, the Kuzari made a version of the same claim in Muslim Spain. So I can't wave it off as a product of the pogroms. It's a real voice in the tradition.

I want to be fair about how it plays out. Chabad's love of every Jew, *ahavat Yisrael*, is real and often beautiful: any Jew who walks in is treated like family. My problem isn't the love. It's the part that says the Jew who behaves badly is still, at the level of the soul, finer than the kindest non-Jew down the street.

Abraham earned his closeness to God. He searched, argued, and brought people close. Can his descendants just inherit it?

**Does the halakha really treat it as birth alone?**

Less than I assumed. The Talmud lists three marks of Abraham's descendants: they are merciful, modest, and do acts of kindness. Someone who has these traits is fit to join this people (Yevamot 79a). Character is in the definition.

The law of tzedakah agrees. Someone who sins on purpose and hasn't repented isn't owed tzedakah as a mitzvah (Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De'ah 251:1). I still want to ask a rav exactly how far that goes. Being Jewish doesn't automatically put you first in line.

And the Torah itself refuses to flatter us. "Not because of your righteousness" do you inherit the land, Moses tells the people, "for you are a stiff-necked people" (Deuteronomy 9:4–6).

But I have to be honest: birth isn't nothing. "A Jew, even when he sins, is still a Jew" (Sanhedrin 44a). You can't resign. So whatever birth gives you, it's real, and it sticks. The question is what it is.

**Then what does birth give you?**

My answer: **membership and obligation, not worth.** Think of a family. You owe your sibling more than you owe a stranger, even on the days they drive you up the wall. That isn't because they're a better person. It's because they're yours, and you can't stop being family. Nobody calls a sibling royalty.

That's the line I want to draw. A family has priority. An aristocracy has rank. The Torah describes us as the first and keeps refusing to let us become the second.

The prophets push hard on this. "Only you have I known of all the families of the earth, *therefore* I will hold you accountable for all your sins" (Amos 3:2). Being chosen comes with a bill. And a few chapters later, Amos goes further: "Are you not like the Ethiopians to Me, O Israel? Did I not bring Israel up from Egypt, and the Philistines from Caphtor?" (Amos 9:7). God has histories with other peoples too.

**So how did American Jews end up split on this?**

Rabbi Akiva says both things in one breath. "Beloved is man, for he was created in the image of God." And then: "Beloved are Israel, for they are called children of God" (Avot 3:14). Universal and particular, side by side. He didn't feel any need to choose.

We did. Look at American Jewish life and you can see the verse broken in half.

- **The universalists** kept "beloved is man." *Tikkun olam*, help everyone. It's inspiring, and it's a real kiddush Hashem. But sometimes the particular gets lost entirely. A homeless Jewish woman I knew in San Diego told me a Jewish agency offered her canned pork. That's one person's story, and I can't verify it. But it captures something: a Jewish institution that forgot it was Jewish.
- **The religious** kept "beloved are Israel." Tzedakah goes to the shul, to Israel, to Jewish poor. The circle stays tight. Meanwhile the halakha itself says to feed the non-Jewish poor, visit their sick, and bury their dead (Gittin 61a). The homeless people of San Diego are "the poor of your city" too.

Each camp is holding half of Rabbi Akiva's sentence and calling it the whole.

**Where does that leave Refa'enu?**

Reframed, not resolved. If Israel is a family, the daily Amidah is a family praying at its own table, for its own first, the way every family does. That's priority, not superiority.

It also isn't the whole liturgy. The same Men of the Great Assembly wrote the Rosh Hashanah Amidah, which asks that all of creation stand in awe of God and become one band. The daily prayer is the family praying. The yearly prayer is the world praying. I'll come back to that.

And there's room inside the blessing itself: you can add a personal prayer in Refa'enu for any specific person who is sick. Whether that includes a non-Jew by name is a question I still want to ask my rav.

Still, I'll be honest. The family frame makes the prayer make sense. It doesn't make the ick fully disappear.
